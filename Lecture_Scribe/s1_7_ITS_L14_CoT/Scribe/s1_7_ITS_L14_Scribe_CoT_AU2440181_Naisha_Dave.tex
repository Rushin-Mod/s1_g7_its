\section*{1. Random Variables and Probability Models}

A \textbf{random variable} is a function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.

Two primary types are used in this tutorial:

\subsection*{Continuous Random Variables}

A random variable $X$ is continuous if it has a \textbf{probability density function (pdf)} $f_X(x)$ such that

\[
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx
\]

and

\[
f_X(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) dx = 1
\]

The \textbf{cumulative distribution function (CDF)} is

\[
F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt
\]

The CDF fully determines the distribution.

\subsection*{Discrete Random Variables}

A discrete random variable has a \textbf{probability mass function (pmf)}

\[
P(X = x)
\]

with

\[
\sum_x P(X=x)=1
\]

\section*{2. Uniform Distribution on an Interval}

A \textbf{uniform distribution} models a situation where every point in an interval is equally likely.

If

\[
X \sim \text{Uniform}(a,b)
\]

then

\[
f_X(x)=
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

and

\[
F_X(x)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x<a \\
\frac{x-a}{b-a}, & a\le x\le b \\
1, & x>b
\end{cases}
\]

\subsection*{Interpretation of ``choosing a point at random on a line segment''}

This means the position $X$ of the chosen point is modeled as

\[
X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,L)
\]

Thus the probability of the point falling in any subinterval is proportional to its length.

If a point divides a segment into two pieces $X$ and $(L-X)$, the analysis of ratios or inequalities between these quantities is performed by expressing the event in terms of the random variable $X$.

\section*{3. Normal Distribution and Likelihood Comparison}

The \textbf{normal distribution} is defined by mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

If

\[
X \sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)
\]

then the density is

\[
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\]

\subsection*{Likelihood-Based Classification}

When an observation could come from two possible normal distributions, classification is based on \textbf{likelihood comparison}.

Suppose an observation $x$ may come from two populations:

\[
f_1(x) \quad \text{and} \quad f_2(x)
\]

If prior probabilities are

\[
P(\text{population 1}) = p_1, \qquad P(\text{population 2}) = p_2
\]

then the posterior comparison uses

\[
p_1 f_1(x) \quad \text{vs} \quad p_2 f_2(x)
\]

This follows from \textbf{Bayes’ theorem}:

\[
P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}
\]

Error probabilities are analyzed by equating the probabilities of misclassification under the two hypotheses.

\section*{4. Minimizing Expected Absolute Deviation}

A common optimization problem in probability is minimizing

\[
E[|X-a|]
\]

for some constant $a$.

\subsection*{Key Result}

The value of $a$ that minimizes $E[|X-a|]$ is the \textbf{median of the distribution of $X$}.

\subsection*{Reasoning}

1. Write

\[
E[|X-a|] = \int |x-a| f_X(x) dx
\]

2. Split the integral at $x=a$

\[
E[|X-a|] =
\int_{-\infty}^{a}(a-x)f_X(x)dx
+
\int_{a}^{\infty}(x-a)f_X(x)dx
\]

3. Differentiate with respect to $a$

4. Set derivative equal to zero

This leads to

\[
P(X \le a) = P(X \ge a)
\]

which defines the \textbf{median}.

\subsection*{Uniform Case}

For

\[
X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,A)
\]

the median equals

\[
A/2
\]

\subsection*{Exponential Case}

For

\[
X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)
\]

the median satisfies

\[
F_X(a)=\frac12
\]

where

\[
F_X(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}
\]

\section*{5. Exponential Distribution}

The exponential distribution models \textbf{waiting times between events}.

If

\[
X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)
\]

then

\[
f_X(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x\ge0
\]

\[
F_X(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}
\]

\subsection*{Mean and Variance}

\[
E[X]=\frac{1}{\lambda}
\]

\[
Var(X)=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}
\]

\subsection*{Memoryless Property}

The exponential distribution satisfies

\[
P(X>s+t \mid X>t)=P(X>s)
\]

This means that the remaining lifetime does not depend on how long the system has already survived.

This property is fundamental when analyzing remaining lifetimes or additional operating time.

\section*{6. Conditional Probability with Mixtures of Exponential Variables}

Sometimes a system may belong to one of several types with different exponential parameters.

Suppose:

Type $i$ battery has lifetime

\[
X_i \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda_i)
\]

The probability of selecting type $i$ is

\[
p_i
\]

Then the lifetime distribution of a randomly selected battery is a \textbf{mixture distribution}.

\subsection*{Survival Probability}

\[
P(X>t)=\sum_i p_i e^{-\lambda_i t}
\]

\subsection*{Conditional Probability of Additional Lifetime}

Using conditional probability,

\[
P(X>t+s \mid X>t)
=
\frac{P(X>t+s)}{P(X>t)}
\]

Substitute the mixture survival probabilities to compute the conditional expression.

\section*{7. Poisson Random Variables}

A \textbf{Poisson distribution} models counts of events occurring in a fixed interval.

If

\[
X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)
\]

then

\[
P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}
\]

\subsection*{Mean and Variance}

\[
E[X]=\lambda
\]

\[
Var(X)=\lambda
\]

\subsection*{Sum of Independent Poisson Variables}

If

\[
X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1), \quad
Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)
\]

and $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then

\[
X+Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)
\]

\subsection*{Conditional Distribution Given the Sum}

If

\[
X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1), \quad
Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)
\]

then conditioning on the total

\[
N=X+Y
\]

gives

\[
X \mid (X+Y=n) \sim \text{Binomial}\left(n,\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)
\]

Thus

\[
P(X=k \mid X+Y=n)
=
\binom{n}{k}
\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^k
\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^{n-k}
\]

\section*{8. Transformations of Random Variables}

If a random variable $Y$ is defined as a function of another variable

\[
Y=g(X)
\]

the distribution of $Y$ can be obtained using either:

\begin{enumerate}
\item CDF method
\item Change of variables for pdf
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{CDF Method}

\[
F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=P(g(X)\le y)
\]

Rewrite the inequality in terms of $X$, then evaluate the probability using the distribution of $X$.

\subsection*{PDF Transformation (One-to-One Case)}

If $Y=g(X)$ is monotonic and differentiable,

\[
f_Y(y)=f_X(x)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right|
\]

where $x=g^{-1}(y)$.

\subsection*{Piecewise Transformations}

If $g(X)$ is not one-to-one, each branch must be handled separately:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Solve $y=g(x)$ for all possible $x$
\item Add densities from each branch.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{9. Approximations for Repeated Independent Trials}

A model of repeated price movements or outcomes can often be described using \textbf{Bernoulli trials}.

Each trial has

\begin{itemize}
\item probability $p$ of success
\item probability $(1-p)$ of failure.
\end{itemize}

Let

\[
S_n = \text{number of successes in } n \text{ trials}
\]

Then

\[
S_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p)
\]

\subsection*{Binomial Probability}

\[
P(S_n=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
\]

\subsection*{Normal Approximation}

For large $n$, the binomial distribution can be approximated by

\[
S_n \approx N(np, np(1-p))
\]

This approximation is useful when $n$ is very large.

\section*{10. Joint Distribution Transformations}

If two independent continuous variables $X$ and $Y$ have densities

\[
f_X(x), \quad f_Y(y)
\]

then the joint density is

\[
f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)
\]

If a new variable is defined by

\[
Z=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}
\]

then determining the distribution of $Z$ requires expressing the probability

\[
P(Z\le z)
\]

in terms of the joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$, typically involving integration over the region

\[
x^2+y^2 \le z^2
\]

This corresponds to integrating over a circular region in the $(x,y)$-plane.

\section*{11. Order Statistics and Maximum of Random Variables}

Let

\[
X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n
\]

be i.i.d. random variables with CDF $F(x)$.

Define

\[
M_n = \max(X_1,\dots,X_n)
\]

\subsection*{Distribution of the Maximum}

The CDF of the maximum is

\[
P(M_n \le x)
=
P(X_1\le x, \dots, X_n\le x)
\]

Because the variables are independent,

\[
F_{M_n}(x) = [F(x)]^n
\]

The pdf is obtained by differentiating.

\subsection*{Joint Distribution of Successive Maxima}

Consider

\[
M_n=\max(X_1,\dots,X_n)
\]

and

\[
M_{n+1}=\max(X_1,\dots,X_n,X_{n+1})
\]

Two cases arise:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $X_{n+1} \le M_n$
\item $X_{n+1} > M_n$
\end{enumerate}

The joint distribution is derived by analyzing these cases using independence and the distribution $F(x)$.
